PROOF

Perceptive Robustness via Out-of-Distribution Fallback for Legged Locomotion

CoRL 2026

1Southern University of Science and Technology2Pengcheng Lab3Tsinghua University4The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou)

TL; DR We boost the robustness of vision-based perceptive locomotion via automatic OOD-triggered proprioceptive behaviour fallback, avoiding policy failures under severe visual degradation, while retaining agile perceptive parkour performance.



Abstract

Vision-based perceptive locomotion has enabled legged robots to achieve agile and terrain-aware locomotion across complex environments. However, the reliability of such systems heavily depends on visual observations, which are inherently susceptible to occlusion, reflective surfaces, and illumination variations, often resulting in catastrophic locomotion failures under degraded perception conditions. In this paper, we present PROOF, a perceptive locomotion framework that enables robust locomotion under severe visual disturbances. Our method adopts a two-stage teacher–student learning paradigm that explicitly models perception reliability: the teacher learns both perception-driven and proprioceptive fallback behaviors, while the student predicts exteroceptive features with uncertainty and suppresses them under out-of-distribution inputs. We evaluate our method on a Unitree Go2 quadruped equipped with a RealSense D435 depth camera in real-world settings. Under normal visual conditions, our method enables the robot to successfully perform high-dynamic parkour maneuvers, including traversing large gaps, elevated platforms, and narrow beam terrains. Under severe visual degradation, such as heavy occlusion and strong illumination changes, the robot continues to maintain stable and robust locomotion performance.

Motivation

Conventional end-to-end perceptive locomotion policies rely heavily on the quality of perception information. Once perception shifts out-of-distribution, degraded or misleading inputs can trigger unsafe behaviors and abrupt policy failures. This sensitivity severely limits their usability in open-world deployment, as shown in the videos below.

Simulation Results

  • Curve on the lower left shows the predicted log-variance that reflects terrain uncertainty.
  • Curve in the lower middle shows the learned gate.
  • Panel on the lower right shows the simulated depth image.
  • For parkour terrains (gap, high platform), no OOD disturbance is injected. For non-parkour terrains (stairs, slopes), we randomly inject vision degradation stronger than that used during training to test how the log-variance and gate respond under more severe visual corruption.
  • For gap and high platform, the learned gate stays open while the predicted log-variance rises as the robot crosses a gap or climbs a high platform, then decreases during regular flat plane.
  • For stair and slope, when strong visual corruption is manually injected, the learned gate switches actively.

We further evaluate robustness under randomized strong visual corruption on non-parkour terrains. Each table entry reports Rvx / S, where Rvx is the mean x-direction velocity tracking reward and S is the traversal success rate over 100 test episodes with 100 parallel environments.

Failure Cases

  • Although our method generalizes to a large extent in the real world, the learned gate can still fail to switch correctly in some scenarios.
  • During continues extreme parkour operation, we observe severe motor heating on the Unitree Go2, which changes motor characteristics and enlarges the sim-to-real gap.
